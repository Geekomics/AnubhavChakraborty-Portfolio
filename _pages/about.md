I am a molecular biologist and genetic engineer with a PhD in Molecular and Integrative Physiology from the University of Kansas Medical Center. During my PhD in discovery and translational kidney research at Dr. Alan Yu's lab, I developed a CRISPR-based gene transactivation strategy for Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease, building on genome engineering, epigenetic editing, reliable cell-based models, and gene delivery. I supported it with quantitative assays that I developed and validated to measure gene expression, protein levels, and cellular responses, much of it in patient-derived primary cells and other hard-to-transduce mammalian cell types. My PhD work has been productive, leading to peer-reviewed papers, a competitive American Heart Association fellowship, talks at national meetings, and a U.S. provisional patent on this approach. I completed my PhD in August 2026 and have since continued as a postdoctoral fellow in Dr. Alan Yu's lab.

My first research experience came from my bachelor’s and master’s dissertation projects. I then spent time as a Senior Research Fellow, where I developed mouse models of acute kidney injury and studied epigenetic changes, specifically DNA methylation during ischemic cell death. My PhD, which came next, is the most extensive work of my career. Along the way, I was fortunate to learn from knowledgeable colleagues and mentors, and when that help was not available, I taught myself and worked through problems systematically until I had a solution.

I independently design, run, analyze, and interpret my experiments. My research experience, particularly my PhD, has given me the ability to identify and turn biological observations into testable hypotheses and to develop experimental strategies that probe the mechanisms behind cellular responses. I care about data I can trust, so I validate my assays with rigorous controls and replicates across independent donors. I learn new methods quickly and am drawn to technologies that make cell studies easier and more reproducible. I think critically, stay steady under pressure, and work well in fast-paced, collaborative research environments where several projects and priorities run at once.

I also enjoy working with people. I have helped other labs with their projects, mentored students who are just starting out, and taken on leadership roles that relied on strong organization, including vice president of a graduate society and planning committee member for a national kidney research symposium. I am comfortable presenting my work, and I like breaking down complex topics into clear, detailed explanations that are easy to follow.

In my postdoctoral work, I am building cellular models, exploring emerging synthetic biology tools, and developing new delivery strategies aimed at advancing gene therapy approaches for Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease. Going forward, I want to keep developing disease models and genetic tools that help us understand disease, and use them to develop therapeutic reagents and translate those into clinically relevant medicines. Years of failed experiments have taught me that failure is part of the process, and that the key is to fail fast so I know when to pivot to a new approach. I put that into practice by testing ideas early, reading negative results carefully, and changing course when the data call for it. I also believe in exploring bold ideas and drawing on new discoveries and technologies that can speed up our understanding of human physiology in disease. I am willing to learn new methods, borrow tools from other fields, and work with people outside my area to test those ideas. I aim to grow into a better scientist and thinker, one who can lead discoveries toward faster and better therapies, ways to prevent disease, or ways to change cell fate.
