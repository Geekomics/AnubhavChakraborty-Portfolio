I am a molecular biologist and genetic engineer with a PhD in Molecular and Integrative Physiology, and my research has primarily focused on kidney diseases. My career began in India, where I developed mouse models of acute kidney injury and studied epigenetic changes, specifically DNA methylation during ischemic cell death. I then moved to the University of Kansas Medical Center, where I completed my PhD in August 2026 and now work as a postdoctoral fellow. My PhD was the most extensive research of my career so far. It centered on genome engineering, building reliable cell-based models, and gene delivery, supported by quantitative assays that I developed and validated to measure gene expression, protein levels, and cellular responses. Much of this work was done in patient-derived primary cells and other hard-to-transduce cell types that vary naturally in how they respond to manipulation. Along the way, I was fortunate to learn from knowledgeable colleagues and mentors, and when that help was not available, I taught myself and worked through problems systematically until I had a solution.

I am skilled in genome and cellular engineering, gene delivery, and disease modeling, from designing the tools to testing them in quantitative assays. I care about data I can trust, so I validate my assays with rigorous controls and replicates across independent donors. I learn new methods quickly and am drawn to technologies that make cell studies easier and more reproducible. I think critically and thrive under pressure. This work has led to peer-reviewed papers, a competitive American Heart Association fellowship, talks at national meetings, and a U.S. provisional patent on a CRISPR activation strategy aimed at treating polycystic kidney disease.

I also enjoy working with people. I have helped other labs with their projects, mentored students who are just starting out, and taken on leadership roles, including vice president of a graduate society and planning committee member for a national kidney research symposium. I like breaking down complex topics into clear, detailed explanations that are easy to follow.

In my postdoctoral work, I am building cellular models, exploring emerging synthetic biology tools, and developing new delivery strategies aimed at advancing gene therapy approaches for Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease. Going forward, I want to keep developing disease models and genetic tools that help us understand disease and turn those findings into clinically relevant work. Years of experiments that did not work have taught me that failure is part of the process, and that the key is to fail fast so I know when to pivot to a new approach. I put that into practice by testing ideas early, reading negative results carefully, and changing course when the data call for it. I also believe in exploring bold ideas and drawing on new discoveries and technologies that can speed up our understanding of human physiology in disease. I am willing to learn new methods, borrow tools from other fields, and work with people outside my area to test those ideas. Done well, this could lead to faster and better therapies, ways to prevent disease, or ways to change cell fate.
